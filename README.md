## Spot copy trading: the simpler starting point

Spot copy trading mirrors purchases and sales of cryptocurrencies without using leverage or margin. If the lead trader buys a supported asset, the system attempts to place a corresponding buy order in your account. When the lead trader sells, your copied position is normally sold as well, although the execution price may differ.

OKX says its spot copy-trading system supports **114 cryptocurrencies as base assets** at the time of the current help-page update. The exact list and minimum order requirements can change, so the asset details shown in the trading interface should be treated as the final reference.

Spot copy trading has several controls worth understanding:

- **Fixed amount:** You allocate the same amount to each copied order.
- **Proportional amount:** Your copied order is calculated from the lead trader’s order value multiplied by your chosen multiplier.
- **Maximum total amount:** New buy orders stop once your total allocation under that trader reaches the limit.
- **Stop loss for trader:** Copying stops when the combined loss from that lead trader reaches your selected threshold.
- **Take profit per order:** A copied order can be closed after reaching your chosen profit level.
- **Stop loss per order:** A copied order can be closed after reaching your chosen loss level.
- **Supported-asset selection:** You can restrict copying to selected cryptocurrencies.

OKX states that spot copy orders generally require sufficient USDT, a supported cryptocurrency, an order above the applicable minimum, and a buy-price difference of no more than 0.5% between the lead trader and copy trader. If the market moves too quickly, a copied order may fail instead of being filled at a much worse price.

That 0.5% rule matters in fast markets. Copy trading does not make execution instantaneous. If a lead trader buys during a sudden price jump, the price available to your account may move outside the permitted range.

### Does spot copy trading use leverage?

No. OKX’s spot copy trading follows standard spot trading rules and does not support leverage or margin. You buy the asset rather than opening a leveraged contract position. That removes liquidation risk caused by leverage, but it does not remove the possibility of losing money when the asset price falls.

Spot copy trading may be more understandable for someone who wants to follow a strategy without dealing with liquidation prices, funding rates, or forced position closures. It is still not passive investing in the traditional sense. You need to monitor the lead trader, the copied assets, and your own allocation.

## Futures copy trading: more flexibility, more ways to lose money

Futures copy trading lets you mirror leveraged futures positions. A lead trader may open a long or short position, use leverage, add risk through multiple positions, and close trades based on market conditions. Your account attempts to follow those actions according to your settings.

The extra flexibility also creates additional failure points:

- Leverage can magnify both profits and losses.
- Funding payments may affect the position result.
- A copied trade can be opened at a different price.
- Your available margin may be insufficient.
- The lead trader may reach a platform risk limit.
- A position can be liquidated before the lead trader’s strategy works as expected.
- A copied order may not be opened if the price difference exceeds OKX’s protection range.

OKX’s copy-trading guide allows users to configure leverage, per-order take profit, per-order stop loss, and a total stop loss for all positions linked to a lead trader. The same guide recommends keeping leverage below 20x, although a lower setting may be more appropriate for users who are still learning how futures work.

A high historical return does not automatically make a futures trader suitable to copy. A trader who produced a large percentage gain may have used aggressive leverage, concentrated positions, or a short period of unusually favorable market conditions.

For a first setup, a more conservative approach is to:

1. Use a small allocation.
2. Set a maximum total amount.
3. Set a total stop-loss threshold.
4. Avoid copying several highly correlated traders.
5. Review whether the trader mostly uses long positions, short positions, or both.
6. Check drawdown and open-position behavior rather than looking only at recent returns.

## How proportional copy trading works

Proportional copy trading calculates your order amount from the lead trader’s order value.

For example, if the lead trader opens a position worth 10,000 USDT and you set a multiplier of 0.1x, your copied order value would be calculated as 1,000 USDT before other platform constraints are applied.

OKX currently documents a proportional multiplier range from **0.01x to 10x** for the relevant futures copy-trading setting. The maximum total amount can be set between **20 USDT and 30,000 USDT**, subject to the rules shown in the account interface.

A proportional setting can make your trades resemble the lead trader’s position sizing more closely, but it can also scale risk faster than expected. If the lead trader’s position size changes sharply, your copied order size can change with it.

The maximum total amount is therefore important. Without a cap, a sequence of copied orders can consume more margin than you intended, particularly when earlier positions remain open.

## How to choose a lead trader on OKX

The marketplace statistics are useful, but they should be read as a group rather than as a leaderboard contest.

Look at:

### Track record length

A trader with a short history may have a very high return because of one favorable market move. A longer record gives you more information about how the trader behaves in different conditions, although it still cannot predict future results.

### Drawdown

Drawdown shows how far the account has fallen from a previous peak. A trader with a high return and a very large drawdown may be taking substantially more risk than the headline return suggests.

### Open positions

Check how many positions remain open and whether the trader tends to hold them for a long time. A high current return can look attractive while unrealized losses remain hidden in open positions.

### Trading style

Some traders make frequent short-term trades. Others hold positions for days or weeks. Your capital and risk controls need to match the strategy. A short-term trader may create more fee and execution exposure, while a leveraged swing trader may expose you to larger overnight price movements.

### Concentration

A trader who repeatedly focuses on one asset or one market direction can perform well until that specific thesis stops working. Diversifying across several traders does not automatically diversify risk if all of them are long the same cryptocurrency.

### Profit-sharing level

The profit-sharing percentage affects your net return. Check the exact percentage shown before confirming the copy relationship rather than assuming every trader uses the same rate.

## Common reasons a copied trade may fail

A copy-trading order may fail even when the lead trader’s order was successful.

Common reasons include:

- Insufficient funds in the trading account.
- The copied order is below the applicable minimum.
- The lead trader has stopped leading trades.
- The lead trader has reached a daily order limit.
- The lead trader triggered a risk-control condition.
- The price difference exceeds the copy-trading protection range.
- Your maximum total allocation has already been reached.
- The contract or cryptocurrency is not supported for copy trading.
- The lead trader’s account does not meet the platform’s requirements.
- Your account or region is not eligible for the feature.

OKX currently states that a copy trader can copy up to 10 trades in `Open/Close` position mode, or one trader in `Buy/Sell` position mode. It also states that lead traders can lead up to 500 trades per day under the documented rules.

These restrictions are not minor technical details. They explain why your account may not match the lead trader’s transaction history, especially when the lead trader opens many positions or trades during sharp price movements.
